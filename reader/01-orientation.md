# Theme 1 — Orientation: the map in ten tips

Before descending into primary research, you need a map. This first reading
is a deliberately practical synthesis written by two people who bridge
research and practice: Neil Brown (researcher on the BlueJ/Blackbox projects)
and Greg Wilson (founder of Software Carpentry, which has taught programming
to thousands of adult scientists). In eight pages it states, with citations,
most of what the rest of this reader will unpack.

Read it first, and then use it as an index: nearly every tip is expanded by a
later article in this reader. Tip 1 (no geek gene) becomes theme 3. Tips 4, 9
and 10 (predictions, novices are not experts, don't just code) become themes
2 and 4. Tip 6 (subgoal-labelled worked examples) becomes theme 5. The tips
on peer instruction and pair programming summarise two practices whose
primary studies did not make this reader's page budget but are worth knowing:
peer instruction roughly halved fail rates across four CS courses in
within-instructor comparisons (Porter et al., 2013, in `articles/extra/`),
and pair programming has meta-analytic support (Umapathy & Ritzhaupt, 2017).

## Reading 1 — Brown & Wilson (2018), Ten quick tips for teaching programming

*PDF: [articles/brown-wilson-2018-ten-quick-tips.pdf](articles/brown-wilson-2018-ten-quick-tips.pdf)
— PLOS Computational Biology, 14(4), e1006023. Open access.*

### Why this article

It is the best short answer available to the question "what does the research
say I should do?" — readable in half an hour, honest about the state of the
evidence, and written for teachers rather than researchers. It also models a
professional attitude the whole reader tries to cultivate: practices are
justified by studies, not by lore, and the authors close by warning against
the gatekeeping culture ("you can't learn programming if you didn't start
young") that theme 3 dismantles empirically.

### How to read it

Read all of it; it is short. Pay particular attention to:

- **Tip 3 (live coding)**: note the *reasons* — it slows the teacher down,
  makes mistakes normal, and transfers process knowledge, not just content.
- **Tip 4 (predictions)**: the counterintuitive evidence that demonstrations
  without a prior prediction teach little or nothing. This one sentence
  should change how you run every demo.
- **Tip 9 (novices are not experts)**: the reading analogy — we do not teach
  reading by handing children a classic novel and going slowly; we use
  shorter books with simpler words and larger print.

Treat the tips as claims to be tested against the rest of the reader, not as
commandments. Some rest on strong evidence (worked examples, peer
instruction), others on weaker or more contested ground (the null result on
context in tip 8 sits uneasily with the retention evidence for media
computation cited in the same tip).

### Guiding questions

1. Which tip surprised you most, and which would meet the most resistance
   from colleagues in your programme? What evidence would you cite?
2. Tip 4 says predictions must be recorded or public before a demo. Design
   the concrete mechanics for this in a lesson you might teach (what do
   students predict, how do they commit, what do you do with wrong
   predictions?).
3. Keep a running table while you work through this reader: for each tip,
   note which later article strengthens, refines, or complicates it. Which
   tips end up with the strongest support?
4. The closing paragraph is about making learners feel they can become
   programmers. Why do the authors treat this as a teaching duty rather than
   a personality trait of the student? (Themes 3 and 7 will answer this;
   write down your current answer first.)
