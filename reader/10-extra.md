# Theme 10 — Extra: going deeper

The six articles below were serious candidates for this reader and were read
in full during its compilation; they were set aside only to keep the
collection at fifteen. Each is referenced somewhere in the theme
introductions, and each rewards reading if you want to go deeper into a
particular theme. The introductions here are deliberately short — enough to
decide whether the article is for you.

## Porter, Bailey Lee & Simon (2013), Halving fail rates using peer instruction

*PDF: [articles/extra/porter-2013-peer-instruction.pdf](articles/extra/porter-2013-peer-instruction.pdf)
— SIGCSE 2013, 177–182. Deepens themes 1 and 5.*

The primary evidence behind Brown & Wilson's tip 2. Across ten years, four
courses and over 10,000 enrolments at UC San Diego, courses taught with peer
instruction (solo clicker vote → peer discussion → re-vote → debrief) had
fail rates 61% lower on average than standard instruction — and 67% lower
when the *same instructor* taught the same course both ways, which rules out
"better teachers simply adopt PI".

Read it for two things: the mechanics of a faithful PI implementation, and
the unusually candid threats-to-validity section (no controlled exams;
pair programming and media computation were adopted simultaneously in CS1).
It is an excellent paper to practise study critique on: decide for yourself
how much of the effect survives the confounds.

## Ben-Ari (1998), Constructivism in computer science education

*PDF: [articles/extra/ben-ari-1998-constructivism.pdf](articles/extra/ben-ari-1998-constructivism.pdf)
— SIGCSE 1998, 257–261. Deepens themes 2 and 4. Note: the PDF is a scan of
the original.*

Five provocative pages that explain why constructivism, taken seriously,
argues *against* discovery learning in programming. Ben-Ari's two claims: a
beginning student, unlike a physics student, brings no effective prior model
of a computer to build on; and the computer forms an "accessible ontological
reality" — flawed mental models are punished immediately and brutally with
bugs, and "there is not much point negotiating models of the syntax or
semantics of a programming language". His conclusions — explicitly teach a
model of the machine, delay coding until a viable model exists, beware
try-it-and-see "bricolage" — anticipate both Sorva's notional-machine
synthesis and the guidance debate of theme 4. Read it as the short, sharp
primary source behind arguments you have already met second-hand.

## Qian & Lehman (2017), Students' misconceptions and other difficulties in introductory programming

*PDF: [articles/extra/qian-lehman-2017-misconceptions.pdf](articles/extra/qian-lehman-2017-misconceptions.pdf)
— ACM Transactions on Computing Education, 18(1), Article 1. Deepens
theme 2.*

A systematic catalogue of what novices get wrong, organised into three
layers — syntactic, conceptual, and strategic knowledge — and, more
usefully, of *where the errors come from*: transfer from natural language
and mathematics, fragile mental models of the machine, missing
problem-solving strategies, unhelpful environments, and teachers' own
knowledge gaps. Where Sorva gives you the theory of misconceptions, this
review gives you the inventory.

Use it instrumentally: when you design diagnostic questions, Investigate
phases for PRIMM lessons, or distractors for peer-instruction questions,
this is the checklist of misconceptions worth targeting. The closing
argument — that identifying misconceptions is a core part of a programming
teacher's pedagogical content knowledge — is a good one-sentence summary of
why this reader exists.

## Ericson et al. (2022), Parsons problems and beyond

*PDF: [articles/extra/ericson-2022-parsons.pdf](articles/extra/ericson-2022-parsons.pdf)
— ITiCSE working group report. Deepens theme 5.*

The definitive treatment of Parsons problems — exercises where students
reorder scrambled code blocks instead of writing code from scratch. Sixteen
years of research, systematically reviewed, support a striking efficiency
claim: solving Parsons problems produces learning gains comparable to
writing the equivalent code, at significantly lower time cost and cognitive
load — time that can be reinvested in more and more varied practice.
Adaptive variants, which adjust difficulty when a student struggles, look
especially promising for keeping weaker students in the productive zone.

This is a long working-group report; read the introduction, the review
findings, and the design recommendations, and treat the rest as reference.
If you adopt one exercise type from it, note the boundary condition: Parsons
problems are a practice and formative tool, not a replacement for ever
writing code.

## Vihavainen, Airaksinen & Watson (2014), A systematic review of approaches for teaching introductory programming and their influence on success

*PDF: [articles/extra/vihavainen-2014-systematic-review.pdf](articles/extra/vihavainen-2014-systematic-review.pdf)
— ICER 2014, 19–26. Deepens themes 3 and 8.*

The source of a number quoted throughout this reader: across 60 before/after
comparisons covering thirteen kinds of teaching intervention, changing the
teaching improved pass rates by 12.8 percentage points on average — roughly
one third of the available room for improvement — and made things worse in
under 10% of cases. Cooperative learning and media computation top the
rankings; game-themed content and assessment tweaks alone sit at the bottom;
the extreme apprenticeship model of theme 8 appears as one of the reviewed
hybrids.

Read it as the quantitative backbone of the claim that outcomes are
substantially under instructor control — and read it critically: the
underlying studies are uncontrolled before/after reports, publication bias
is likely, and the authors are appropriately careful about comparing
intervention types head-to-head.

## Quille & Bergin (2019), CS1: how will they do? How can we help?

*PDF: [articles/extra/quille-bergin-2019-press.pdf](articles/extra/quille-bergin-2019-press.pdf)
— Computer Science Education, 29(2–3), 254–282. Deepens themes 3 and 7.*

Thirteen years of one research programme: PreSS, the best-validated model
for predicting, about 10% of the way into a first programming course, which
students are at risk. Three factors carry the model — programming
self-efficacy (the dominant one), prior mathematics performance, and hours
spent gaming — and a multi-institutional revalidation across eleven
institutions in two countries (n = 692), including community colleges,
still identified weak students with 78% sensitivity a decade after the
model was built.

Two reasons to read it beyond the headline. It is a rare example of the
field revalidating its own instruments (the review of abandoned prediction
models in section 2 is quietly damning), and the authors are explicit about
ethics: prediction exists to trigger *support* — their interventions
include a Scratch on-ramp and growth-mindset messaging — never selection.
If your programme ever considers an intake test, this paper is the evidence
base for insisting on that distinction.
