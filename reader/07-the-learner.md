# Theme 7 — The learner: self-efficacy and the invisible course

Cognitive design is necessary but not sufficient. A large fraction of
attrition in programming courses is affective, and the pivotal variable is
*self-efficacy*: a student's belief in their own capability to program
(Bandura, 1997). Programming self-efficacy predicts course outcomes more
strongly than most cognitive background variables, and in the best-validated
early-prediction model for CS1 it is the dominant factor (Quille & Bergin,
2019, in `articles/extra/` — a model whose authors insist prediction should
trigger *support*, never selection). Crucially, self-efficacy is not a fixed
trait: it is built and destroyed by the texture of early experiences, with
mastery experiences — succeeding at something and attributing the success to
yourself — as its strongest source.

That last clause is where this theme's article earns its place. What
determines self-efficacy is not success itself but *how the student
interprets* the success — and those interpretations happen at home, at
23:00, invisible to you. The one study in this theme follows students into
that invisible course. Read it with theme 3 in mind (momentum: early
experiences compound) and notice how many earlier design decisions — many
small early wins, worked examples students can complete, error messages that
do not humiliate, no public rankings — turn out to be self-efficacy
interventions whether or not anyone called them that.

## Reading 11 — Kinnunen & Simon (2012), My program is ok — am I? Computing freshmen's experiences of doing programming assignments

*PDF: [articles/kinnunen-simon-2012-my-program-is-ok.pdf](articles/kinnunen-simon-2012-my-program-is-ok.pdf)
— Computer Science Education, 22(1), 1–28.*

### Why this article

Because it shows you what you will never see from the front of the room.
Nine first-year students were interviewed repeatedly across a ten-week
course about the experience of doing programming assignments. From the
interviews the authors build a six-stage anatomy of an assignment (getting
started, encountering difficulties, dealing with difficulties, succeeding,
submitting, stopping), each with characteristic emotional events: "It's
Greek to me", being "hit by lightning" (an error arriving while confident),
the "hamster wheel" (repeating actions without using feedback), success that
feels like winning a lottery rather than an achievement.

The central finding: students run a near-continuous *self-efficacy
assessment* alongside the assignment, fed by expectations (how long "should"
this take?), comparisons with peers, and partner dynamics — and this
assessment can decouple from actual performance. The paper's four narratives
include the two that should haunt course designers: students who *succeed*
at the assignment yet conclude they are inadequate ("severe hindsight" —
the success was luck, the partner's work, or too slow), and students who
struggle yet maintain confidence because they can place their difficulty in
a trajectory ("I will get there eventually"). A completed assignment is not
evidence that anything good happened to the learner.

Methodologically this is the reader's exemplar of qualitative research done
carefully: a grounded-theory study with nine participants that does not
pretend to count anything, and instead delivers categories and mechanisms
you can observe in your own students next week.

### How to read it

Read sections 1–2 briskly (note how the authors justify rigour in a
nine-person study — useful when you evaluate qualitative work later). Slow
down at section 3: Table 1 (the stages and their emotions) and the
narratives in section 3.2.2 are the heart. Read the "severe hindsight" and
"I will get there eventually" narratives twice, and the discussion of
expectations and peer comparison closely — that is where the design
implications live. As you read, translate each phenomenon into a moment you
have experienced yourself as a learner; you have been on the hamster wheel.

### Guiding questions

1. Explain how a student can complete an assignment successfully and end up
   with *lower* self-efficacy. List the attribution mechanisms involved
   (luck, partner, time-compared-to-peers, hindsight) and one countermeasure
   for each.
2. Which signals in a course you know invite peer comparison (visible
   progress dashboards, who-finished-first lab culture, public
   leaderboards)? For each: keep, redesign, or remove — and why?
3. Design help-seeking so that getting help still produces a mastery
   experience: what should a lab assistant do and *not* do when unsticking a
   student? Connect to scaffolding (theme 8's extreme apprenticeship rule
   that instructors give hints, never solutions).
4. The "hit by lightning" experience is triggered by feedback arriving while
   the student is confident. How do the feedback-design choices of theme 6
   (message content, tone, timing) look when you re-read them through this
   affective lens?
