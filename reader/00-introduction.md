# Introduction to this reader

You are preparing to teach programming to beginners. That sentence contains a
trap: because you can program, it is tempting to believe you already know what
your students will go through. You do not — and that is not a personal
failing but a well-documented phenomenon. Experts have automated away the very
steps novices struggle with, and can no longer see them. The fifteen articles
in this reader are chosen to give those steps back to you: to show what
learning to program actually demands, what teaching approaches demonstrably
help, and what any of this means in the setting where you will teach —
practice-oriented programmes for young adults, many of whom arrive with
setbacks, jobs, and doubts about whether they belong.

## Why these fifteen

Introductory programming ("CS1" in the research literature) is one of the most
heavily studied subjects in education. Five decades of research have produced
a few results that every programming teacher should know first-hand rather
than second-hand. This reader collects the primary sources for those results:

- Students finishing a programming course often cannot read or trace simple
  code, and reading skill underpins writing skill (Lister et al., 2004).
- Novices must build a mental model of an invisible machine, and most classic
  misconceptions live exactly there (Sorva, 2013).
- Programming grades are not bimodal, and belief in a "geek gene" is folklore
  with measurable costs (Patitsas et al., 2020); the real mechanism behind
  extreme outcomes is momentum, which teachers can influence (Robins, 2010).
- For novices learning a cognitively overwhelming skill, strong guidance
  beats discovery (Kirschner et al., 2006), and the field has translated that
  into a concrete, sequenced instructional theory (Xie et al., 2019) and
  classroom designs with evidence behind them (Sentance et al., 2019;
  Margulieux et al., 2020).
- Feedback is where learning happens, and automated feedback mostly is not
  yet the feedback students need (Keuning et al., 2018).
- A large share of failure is affective, and it is invisible from the front
  of the classroom (Kinnunen & Simon, 2012).
- Vocational and short-cycle programmes have their own evidence base, both
  for course design (Vihavainen et al., 2011) and for the institutional
  obstacles their students face (Lyon & Denner, 2019).
- Generative AI has invalidated much of traditional assessment and shifted
  the core skill from writing code to judging it — with the greatest risks
  falling on the weakest students (Denny et al., 2024; Prather et al., 2024).

Woven through the selection are deliberately different perspectives:
cognitive-psychology experiments, large-scale statistical studies, a
simulation, qualitative interview studies, a systematic review, working-group
reports, and practitioner syntheses. Part of learning to teach is learning to
read this variety of evidence critically.

## How to read education research

A few calibration notes before you start.

**Context travels badly.** Most of these studies were done in university CS1
courses, often in the US or Northern Europe. Your students in a graduaat
programme differ: more vocational motivation, more non-traditional routes,
more external constraints. Some findings transfer directly, some need
re-weighting, a few may not hold. Each introduction flags this where it
matters. Treat "does this transfer to my context?" as a standing question.

**Effect sizes and designs matter more than conclusions.** You will meet
quasi-experiments without random assignment, correlations dressed as
hierarchy claims, an influential theory evaluated on nine participants, and a
retrospective study of pass rates with honest pages of validity threats.
None of this makes the work worthless — it makes it evidence to weigh rather
than truth to cite. The guiding questions regularly ask you to critique the
design, because that is a professional skill for a teacher: you will spend a
career being sold pedagogical interventions.

**Old is not obsolete.** The oldest article here reports data from 2004; the
core difficulties of novice programmers have been stable since the 1980s.
What changes is tooling and context — most dramatically since 2022, which is
why the reader ends with two articles on generative AI and why that theme
should be read as a first draft of history rather than a settled account.

## A note on the thread that connects everything

If you retain one idea from this reader, make it this: **comprehension comes
before production**. Students must read, trace, and explain code before they
can reliably write it, yet traditional courses demand writing from week one
and assess little else. This finding appears in nearly every theme — it
motivates the reading-first lesson designs, the mixed assessment formats, the
worked examples, and it is also the skill that survives the arrival of AI
assistants, which make code cheap and judgement expensive. The second idea to
retain: **early experiences compound**. Success and failure in the first
weeks snowball, cognitively (Robins) and emotionally (Kinnunen & Simon), and
generative AI can widen that gap further (Prather et al.). Almost everything
in this reader is, one way or another, a technique for getting the snowball
rolling in the right direction.
